Longmont adds $36.3 million to 1st and Main transit center

The council also approved a development agreement with Main Street Tebo for 246 apartments and a garage of more than 700 spaces beside the RTD bus terminal.

Longmont will add $36.3 million, most of it borrowed, to its 1st and Main transit center, debt backed by tax revenue from the development.

The City Council voted unanimously Tuesday to approve a Phase II development agreement with Main Street Tebo LLC for the site at 1st Avenue and Main Street. The agreement covers a bus terminal with 10 bays, more than 700 parking spaces, about 246 apartments and roughly 14,000 square feet of commercial space the city expects to lease.

The total project cost is $57 million. The Regional Transportation District is contributing $16,402,510. The rest comes from city funds, a $4 million state transit grant and downtown tax increment.

The capital money came through the consent agenda. Council members added $36,336,106 to the capital program for the transit station area, including $31,489,873 from certificates of participation, a form of city debt.

A second ordinance, the 14th appropriation of 2026, added $37,008,731 and put the year's total budget at $799,196,139. The largest piece of that covers the same transit project.

Molly O'Donnell, the city's housing director, told the council that 350 of the garage's roughly 700 spaces are leased to the apartment building, 200 are reserved for RTD and 150 are open to the public.

City Manager Harold Dominguez said the debt sold with AA+ ratings at a 5.1 percent interest rate, inside the range the council had set. He said the city restructured the financing so the entire issue is tax-exempt, which lowered the interest cost.

The agreement also commits the city to reimburse the developer up to $600,000 for right-of-way improvements, paid from the Street Fund.

Council members also exempted the project from the city's 1 percent art requirement after a public hearing. Instead of paying about $110,000 into the citywide art fund, the project sets aside $200,000 for a mural on the transit center's south wall.

Angela Brill, the city's Art in Public Places administrator, said the mural is about 5,000 square feet. She said it will still go through the city's artist selection process.

Council Member Jake Marsing moved to add a distinct local preference to the call for entry. The amendment passed 5 to 2, with Diane Crist and Mayor Susie Hidalgo-Fahring opposed, and the resolution then passed unanimously.

Work on the transit center is under way this fall. One piece is still pending: an ordinance vacating public utility easements at 11 South Main Street passed its first reading Tuesday and returns Nov. 3.

ALSO AT THE MEETING

The council gave first reading to the Longmont Gateway Northwest annexation, 26.11 acres at 12927 and 12775 N. 107th Street at U.S. Highway 287 and Park Ridge Avenue. The concept plan allows about 157 to 470 homes.

At least half of the homes must sit on individually platted lots or condominium units. At least 75 percent of the lower-density area must be ownership units, and that carried 5 to 2 with Matthew Popkin and the mayor opposed.

Buildings along U.S. 287 cannot run more than 175 linear feet without a break. That carried 4 to 3 with Popkin, Alex Kalkhofer and the mayor opposed.

A motion to require preservation of the historic Thompson farmhouse failed 3 to 4. Council members approved traffic calming for the neighborhoods to the west and public pedestrian access to any park on the site, both without opposition.

The applicant is NLA Investors LLC. The Planning and Zoning Commission recommended approval 5 to 2 on June 17, and second reading is set for Nov. 3.

The council also annexed 4.117 acres at 11008 Airport Road, east of Airport Road and north of 9th Avenue, and zoned it residential mixed neighborhood for a concept plan of 27 to 44 homes. The eligibility resolution passed 6 to 1 with Sean McCoy opposed, and the annexation ordinance passed 5 to 2 with McCoy and Hidalgo-Fahring opposed.

Two conditions from the Oct. 6 first reading were rewritten. Traffic calming must be installed to the maximum extent feasible as approved by the development review committee, which passed without opposition. A fire mitigation and vegetation plan is due March 1, 2027, with dead or dying trees removed by Aug. 1, 2027, which passed 6 to 1 with Crystal Prieto opposed.

Ten residents of Champion Greens and the neighboring Greens subdivision spoke against the annexation. They raised drainage and groundwater, cut-through traffic on Champion Circle and Doral Drive, tree removal and the density of the plan.

The city set its property tax levy at 13.420 mills for 2027, the same rate as last year, and the Downtown Development Authority set its levy at 5.000 mills, also unchanged.

The General Improvement District No. 1 board set its levy at 6.798 mills, unchanged as well. Staff told the council that a 2.44-mill public safety ballot measure would require amending the city ordinance if it passes.

A thirteenth appropriation added $492,736 across five funds and put the 2026 budget at $762,187,408 before the transit appropriation. It includes $28,736 for parks vandalism repairs and $16,367 for Longmont Housing Authority accounting. Another $800 covers police training reimbursement.

Members directed the city manager to run an interview process for the new technology policy advisory board and finish it by Dec. 4. The process sets a council survey for draft questions, a two-member review committee to shortlist up to 15 candidates, interviews of up to 20 minutes, recorded sessions and two alternates.

Thirty-two people have applied for seven seats, and Crist and Popkin volunteered to sit on the interview committee.

A 6 to 1 vote remanded a garage variance at 545 Collyer Street to the Planning and Zoning Commission, with Popkin opposed. The commission had approved a reduction of the side setback from 5 feet to zero on Sept. 16 by 6 to 1 for the Bemis-Rowan House, a designated landmark, and neighbor Sharon O'Leary appealed.

Before the vote, the council accepted additional evidence from both the appellant and the property owner without opposition. Members then directed the commission to weigh the self-imposed hardship criterion and seek evidence about whether the garage demolition was an error.

Two ordinances passed without discussion. One amended the municipal code's election section to match state law, and the other abolished the Golf Course Advisory Board.

Members conditionally approved a vehicle sales and rental site plan at 206 South Main Street. The franchisee said rental cars will be washed at one of four car washes within 2 miles rather than on site, answering a question the Planning and Zoning Commission forwarded to the council.

The rest of the consent agenda passed in one vote. It included 2027 operating plans for the Tourism Improvement District, the Harvest Moon Business Improvement District and the LFM Business Improvement District.

Also on that list were an amendment to the downtown development authority agreement, reappointment of Mohammad Islam and Sean McCoy to the tourism board, a roof improvement amendment and acceptance of seven amendments the city manager had already approved.

Three people spoke during the first public comment period. One urged the council to weigh surveillance products sold by Axon, and one described a second city-funded fruit tree hub planned for 2027. A third promoted a Visible Government League meeting and walked through her reading of the statewide ballot measures.

Council members closed with announcements. The museum drew 1,976 visitors on the Saturday of its reopening, and the youth council holds a trunk-or-treat Oct. 23 at 4:30 p.m.

A joint session with the school board is set for Oct. 21 at 6 p.m. at the senior center. The Day of the Dead celebration is Oct. 24 at 11 a.m. at 4th Avenue and Kimbark Street.
