### 1. Council votes to bring marijuana hospitality rules back for consideration

**Score:** 17/20 · **Advance** · Official sources: [September 22 council recording, motion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jhsFsEz0P5A&t=1444s), [recording, vote](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jhsFsEz0P5A&t=2195s), [September 22 budget packet, prior-policy explanation](https://longmont.primegov.com/Public/CompiledDocument?meetingTemplateId=16823&compileOutputType=1)

During the “future agenda items” portion of the September 22 council meeting, a member moved to direct the city manager and attorney to bring back a revised marijuana hospitality ordinance on a timeline consistent with staff’s work plan. The motion called for retail marijuana hospitality **and sales** licenses, and for removing authorization for hospitality licenses without a retail sales component. Council approved that direction **4–3**. The auto-generated transcript identifies three members in opposition: Diane Crist, Matthew Popkin and Crystal Prieto. This was a vote to prepare legislation for later consideration, **not** a vote to permit on-site consumption or to issue a license. Staff discussed doing the work after the November election; the motion did not establish a firm return date.

The budget packet explains why a new ordinance matters. Council previously authorized up to four hospitality licenses, but an implementation measure failed in 2025. The packet says no such licenses have been issued because current rules allow a license category while prohibiting product consumption at the licensed premises. It estimates up to $8,000 annually in licensing fees if all four licenses were issued; any sales tax revenue is unknown. Speakers at the September 22 meeting raised competing concerns about city revenue, spaces for consumption, secondhand smoke and safety. Those points are arguments made in council discussion, not established forecasts or health findings. The **dispensary annexation vote was a separate earlier action**: it concerned existing retailers in Boulder County enclaves, not this proposal for hospitality establishments.

**Why it matters:** A closely split council has revived a policy that stalled last year. Residents and prospective operators need to know that legal on-site consumption remains unresolved while staff drafts a new measure.

**Plain-language brief:** Longmont’s council voted 4–3 on September 22 to ask staff to bring marijuana hospitality rules back for another look. The proposed approach would focus on businesses that pair on-site hospitality with retail sales. Council has **not** approved those businesses to open or allowed on-site consumption. A city budget memo says earlier rules created up to four possible licenses, but a later measure needed to make the businesses work did not pass, and no licenses have been issued. Members disagreed over possible tax revenue, public use, smoke exposure and safety. Staff discussed returning after the November election, but council set no firm date. This vote is separate from the earlier annexation of existing dispensaries. The next decision will come when a new ordinance is published and council votes on its wording. [Sources: council recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jhsFsEz0P5A&t=1444s); [budget packet](https://longmont.primegov.com/Public/CompiledDocument?meetingTemplateId=16823&compileOutputType=1).

**Reporter next step:** Obtain the written motion and final vote record from the clerk. Ask staff for the draft ordinance, timeline and analysis of the sales-only choice; interview prospective applicants and opponents, and independently check health and revenue claims.

### 2. Four Longmont measures are set for the November ballot

**Score:** 16/20 · **Advance** · Official source: [City Clerk’s ballot measure page](https://longmontcolorado.gov/city-clerk/election-information/november-3-2026-election-ballot-measures/)

The City Clerk says council referred four questions to the November 3 coordinated election. Ballots are expected to arrive in early October. Question 3A asks voters to authorize a public safety tax increase estimated at $19.5 million in its first full year. Its mechanisms are a property tax increase of no more than 2.44 mills, subject to the measure’s adjustment language, and a 0.33 percentage point increase in the city sales and use tax. The stated uses include police, fire, 911 dispatch, equipment and facilities. The tax provisions would begin in 2027 **if voters approve them**. The amount after the first year depends on collections and the ballot language; $19.5 million is not a permanent annual cap.

Question 3B would add open space to city lands that generally cannot be conveyed without voter approval, with listed exceptions for roads, utilities, essential infrastructure, operations and structures. Question 3C would allow ranked voting methods, where possible, for mayor and council elections beginning in November 2029. Question 3D would change the election cycle for ward and at-large council seats, but its operation is expressly contingent on voter approval of 3C. The prior beat file tracked ranked choice voting as a study-session issue in March; ballot placement is the verified new development. No election result exists yet, and the city’s ballot descriptions should not be substituted for interviews with supporters, opponents and election administrators.

**Why it matters:** Voters face a direct decision about city revenue, land disposition and how future councils are elected. The dependency between 3C and 3D deserves a clear ballot explainer.

**Plain-language brief:** Longmont voters will see four city questions on the November 3 ballot. One asks whether to raise property and sales taxes for police, fire and emergency services. The city estimates that change would bring in $19.5 million in the first full year. Another question would require voter approval before the city sells or transfers most open space land. A third would allow ranked choice voting for city elections starting in 2029 where possible. A fourth would change when ward and at-large council seats are elected, but only if the ranked choice question passes. Ballots are expected in early October. These are proposals for voters to decide; none has passed. Read the full wording before voting, especially the conditions and exceptions. [Source: City Clerk](https://longmontcolorado.gov/city-clerk/election-information/november-3-2026-election-ballot-measures/).

**Reporter next step:** Obtain the county TABOR notice and election administration guidance; ask city finance staff for household tax examples and both sides for their case on each question. Verify how “where possible” would work for 3C.

### 3. Council creates technology policy advisory board as city pauses fixed ALPR replacements

**Score:** 16/20 · **Advance** · Official sources: [September 22 packet](https://longmont.primegov.com/Public/CompiledDocument?meetingTemplateId=16823&compileOutputType=1), [meeting recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jhsFsEz0P5A&t=4700s), [September 17 city ALPR update](https://longmontcolorado.gov/news/community-update-on-automated-license-plate-reader-alpr-technology/)

At its September 22 meeting, council approved ordinance O-2026-58, establishing a technology policy advisory board. The packet describes a seven-member voting board with a youth council appointee who does not vote. Its authority is advisory: it can recommend policy, but the ordinance does not give it direct control over police equipment or city systems. The listed subjects include resident data, privacy, artificial intelligence, camera technology, automated license plate readers, facial recognition and autonomous vehicles. This breadth makes the board’s appointments and first work plan consequential. The recording transcript indicates final approval; the packet alone contains the proposed text.

Separately, a September 17 city update says Public Safety removed 15 fixed Flock license plate reader cameras after contracting with Axon. It has delayed deployment of 15 replacement Axon cameras while reviewing policies, data sharing, audits, documentation and the changing legal environment. The city did **not** say all forms of license plate reading have ended. The ALPR decision and the new advisory board are connected by policy subject, but the sources do not establish that the board caused the pause or will decide when replacements go up. The board’s actual remit, access to information and appointment process merit reporting, as do the safeguards the city adopts before any fixed cameras return.

**Why it matters:** Residents can now watch a defined advisory process for city technology policy while a particular surveillance deployment remains on hold.

**Plain-language brief:** Longmont’s council approved a new board to advise it on city technology rules. The board can study topics such as privacy, artificial intelligence and cameras, including license plate readers. Its role is to make recommendations; council and city staff still make the decisions. In a separate update, the city said it removed 15 fixed Flock license plate reader cameras and has delayed installing 15 replacement Axon cameras. Public Safety says it is reviewing how the system would be used, checked and shared. The city has not announced a date for the replacements. The new board and the camera pause are related topics, but the city has not said the board will decide the camera question. Watch for appointments, meeting dates and the results of the city’s review. [Sources: council packet](https://longmont.primegov.com/Public/CompiledDocument?meetingTemplateId=16823&compileOutputType=1); [city update](https://longmontcolorado.gov/news/community-update-on-automated-license-plate-reader-alpr-technology/).

**Reporter next step:** Confirm the final adopted ordinance and appointment timeline with the clerk; request the Axon contract, current ALPR policy and audit/data-sharing rules. This technology lead needs extra review of what the policy actually permits before any public-facing claim about privacy effects.

### 4. Council authorizes purchase of the YMCA property at 950 Lashley Street

**Score:** 14/20 · **Advance** · Official sources: [September 22 agenda and packet](https://longmont.primegov.com/Portal/Meeting?meetingTemplateId=16821), [meeting recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jhsFsEz0P5A&t=4100s)

The September 22 council consent vote authorized an agreement for the city to buy the YMCA property at 950 Lashley Street and approved a related childcare lease. The packet puts the total purchase price at **$4,513,469**: $4.5 million for the property plus $13,469 for equipment and closing costs. Recreation impact fees are identified as the funding source. Staff described a September 30 closing target, subject to due diligence and agreement requirements. The vote therefore authorizes the purchase process; it does not prove title has transferred. The packet also estimates about $2.07 million in annual operating expense in 2026 dollars and says full operating cost recovery is not expected before at least year four. Later capital work would require further decisions and funding.

The childcare lease is designed to let the YMCA continue preschool and before/after-school programs in designated space after city acquisition. It runs through June 4, 2027, at $5,000 monthly rent and contains early termination terms. Those services therefore depend on the acquisition and the lease’s actual implementation. A separate Growing Gardens farm lease at the site was introduced on first reading at the same meeting and was scheduled for a later final reading; it should not be described as enacted. Taken together, these documents show the city pursuing public recreation capacity while trying to preserve existing community uses during a transition.

**Why it matters:** This is a multimillion-dollar property decision with near-term consequences for recreation and childcare access. Closing and service continuity are the facts to verify next.

**Plain-language brief:** Longmont’s council approved an agreement to buy the YMCA property at 950 Lashley Street for about $4.51 million, including equipment and closing costs. The city has not yet confirmed that the sale has closed. Staff is aiming for September 30, with due diligence and other requirements still to finish. Council also approved a lease meant to keep YMCA childcare programs in part of the building after the city buys it. The lease would run through June 4, 2027, at $5,000 a month, and either side has some rights to end it early. The city also began considering a separate farm lease for Growing Gardens, but that measure still needs a final vote. The next question is whether the purchase closes and how the programs operate afterward. [Sources: council packet](https://longmont.primegov.com/Public/CompiledDocument?meetingTemplateId=16823&compileOutputType=1); [recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jhsFsEz0P5A).

**Reporter next step:** Check the property record and city announcement after September 30; ask the city and YMCA about program schedules, capacity and transition notices. Confirm whether the Growing Gardens ordinance passes its second reading on October 6.

### 5. 21st Avenue rail crossing is scheduled for two closure periods

**Score:** 13/20 · **Advance** · Official source: [City construction notice](https://longmontcolorado.gov/news/21st-ave-railroad-construction-start/)

The city announced that railroad quiet-zone construction at 21st Avenue is scheduled to begin September 28. The notice anticipates a complete vehicle crossing closure until about October 9, with a temporary reopening from roughly October 10 through November 2. A second complete closure is scheduled from November 3 to November 24. Pedestrian access along the north side of the crossing is also affected. The city describes a detour using Collyer Street, 17th Avenue and Alpine Street. Because this is work around an active rail line, travelers should follow posted controls rather than assume an exact reopening time from the advance notice.

The long-term goal is a quiet zone, which would reduce routine train horn sounding at the crossing after the necessary physical changes and federal approval. The city says it expects Federal Railroad Administration approval in summer 2027. That is an expectation, not an approval already granted. It also does not mean trains stop using the crossing or horns become impossible: safety rules and emergencies can still require horns. This lead is unusually immediate for residents because the first closure is only days away. The best service journalism would map the detour, confirm pedestrian and transit routes, and update the dates if weather or rail coordination changes the schedule.

**Why it matters:** The crossing closure changes daily travel for drivers and pedestrians before the larger quiet-zone project is complete.

**Plain-language brief:** Work on the 21st Avenue railroad crossing is scheduled to start September 28. The city says cars will not be able to use the crossing until about October 9. It expects to reopen the road for a few weeks, then close it again from November 3 through about November 24. A pedestrian route on the north side will also close during work. The posted detour uses Collyer Street, 17th Avenue and Alpine Street. These dates may shift during construction, so check city updates before traveling. The work is part of a plan to create a railroad quiet zone. That quiet zone still needs federal approval, which the city expects in summer 2027. Trains will continue to use the line. [Source: City construction notice](https://longmontcolorado.gov/news/21st-ave-railroad-construction-start/).

**Reporter next step:** Confirm the actual closure start on September 28, photograph posted detours, and check whether school buses, emergency access and nearby businesses have special arrangements.

### 6. Proposed 2027 budget includes Ride Longmont expansion and sanitation service changes

**Score:** 12/20 · **Advance** · Official source: [September 22 council budget packet](https://longmont.primegov.com/Public/CompiledDocument?meetingTemplateId=16823&compileOutputType=1)

The budget material presented to council proposes several changes residents would notice in 2027. For Ride Longmont microtransit, staff writes that two additional vehicles and longer service hours are expected to begin in January 2027 to address wait times and demand. The packet also says the city is seeking a Colorado Clean Transit Enterprise grant; that award is pending. The presence of an expansion in a proposed budget is not a final service schedule or a grant decision. A useful follow-up is the exact hours, vehicle procurement status, cost and whether riders in each service area would see shorter waits.

On sanitation, staff proposes suspending the scheduled spring branch collection because of low participation, adding another fall leaf collection round, and retaining funds for storm-related citywide branch cleanup when needed. It says trash and composting rate options will return in October. The packet lists proposed Sanitation Fund expenses of $13.26 million. It also lists proposed Water Fund expenses of $51.01 million, up from $46.35 million in 2026, including a $9.07 million draw on fund balance largely for capital projects. Those are budget proposals, not an adopted rate increase. The story should separate a proposed service change from a rate decision, explain the effect on typical households, and track amendments through council’s budget process.

**Why it matters:** The proposal affects how people get around and which curbside services they receive, while larger utility spending raises questions for budget coverage.

**Plain-language brief:** Longmont’s proposed 2027 budget calls for two more Ride Longmont vehicles and longer service hours starting in January. Staff says the changes could help reduce waits, and the city is seeking a state grant to help pay for the service. The budget also proposes ending the scheduled spring branch pickup and adding another fall leaf pickup. The city would keep money available for a larger branch cleanup after storms. These plans are in a budget proposal; council has not finished making its 2027 decisions. Trash and composting rate options are expected to return in October. Residents should watch for the final budget, an actual transit schedule and any rate vote before treating the changes as certain. [Source: council budget packet](https://longmont.primegov.com/Public/CompiledDocument?meetingTemplateId=16823&compileOutputType=1).

**Reporter next step:** Get the 2027 budget calendar and adopted version; ask staff for the specific transit hours, sanitation participation data and household rate examples when options appear in October.
